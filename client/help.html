face Getting started

Your first stop is the avatar creator. Pick a display name and shape your look — skin tone, body type, hair, beard, glasses, clothes, and an accessory. Every option is a simple toggle, and the preview panel on the left renders your choices live, so the character you build is exactly the one that walks into the room a moment later. There's no "confirm" step buried in a menu: once you've typed a name, the Enter the Lounge button lights up and you're ready to go.

After that you land in the room chooser, a panel listing every room currently on the server. You can filter it by topic tag or access type, sort by newest or most active, join an existing room, or spin up a brand-new one of your own with a name, tags, and a starting visual style. Closing the chooser without picking anything simply leaves you in the default Lobby — a safe, always-on space to get your bearings, chat, and spar before you go exploring.

lightbulb Don't know where to start? Join Leonardo's Workshop for a guided tour of a Renaissance-themed educational room, or The Alchemist's Vault for a timed escape room — both ship with the server and are covered step by step later in this guide.

directions_walk Moving around

There are two ways to get around, and both are equally valid — use whichever feels natural:

  • Arrow keys for continuous, game-like directional control. Hold a key to keep walking; release it to stop.
  • Click anywhere on the floor to walk there. Your avatar paths toward the spot and steers around furniture automatically, so you don't have to thread the needle between chairs and tables yourself.

Movement is bounded by the room walls and aware of every piece of furniture's collision box, so you can move naturally around the space without clipping through the scenery. Rooms are also bigger than a single screen: most rooms are made of several connected tiles arranged in a grid, and walking to the edge of one tile carries you through a doorway into the next. A small mini-map in the HUD always shows which tile you're standing on and which neighboring tiles exist, so you never lose track of where you are in a multi-room build.

chair Objects & actions

Click an interactive object — a chair, a bookshelf, a puzzle prop, an AI character — and a radial action wheel opens right where you clicked, listing every action that object supports. The available actions depend entirely on the object: you can sit on a sofa, lounge, browse a bookshelf, watch a video, play a track, talk to a character, or solve a puzzle, all from the same simple menu.

Your avatar walks to the object's anchor point before the action plays out — actions are attached to real places in the room rather than to a global menu, so a conversation, a puzzle, or a nap on the sofa all happen exactly where the object sits in the space.

chat_bubble Chatting

Public messages show up in the chat panel and as short-lived speech bubbles above your avatar, so conversation stays visible in the space itself rather than being tucked away in a sidebar only. Anyone nearby can follow along without even glancing at the chat log. Private messages work the same way to send, but stay strictly one-to-one between you and the person you sent them to — nobody else in the room sees them, in the log or as a bubble.

Switch between the two with the Public / Private toggle above the chat input. If you flag or report a message, that action is scoped to the message itself, so moderators can act on a specific line without needing more context than what's already on screen.

sports_mma Combat

Sparring is an optional mode layered directly into the room — there is no separate game screen, no matchmaking queue, and no loading transition. Anyone in the room can throw a punch at any time, and anyone nearby can get pulled in.

  • SpacePunch
  • Ctrl / CmdKick
  • BHold to block

Your attack and defense animations play immediately for responsiveness, while the actual outcome is decided by the server based on range, cooldown, stamina, and stun state — so a lucky-looking hit on your screen still has to actually land server-side, and fights stay fair for everyone in the room instead of rewarding whoever has the least lag.

smart_toy Every room keeps a resident bot around specifically so there's always someone to test combat mechanics against, even in an otherwise empty room. It moves, taunts in public chat, blocks, and attacks back like a real opponent.

meeting_room Rooms

The Change Room pill sits permanently in the room HUD next to the online-player count. Open it any time — mid-conversation, mid-fight, it doesn't matter — to browse or create rooms without reloading the page or losing your avatar.

  • Filter the room list by topic tag, access type (all / public / invite), and sort order (newest or most active), so a busy server stays easy to search.
  • Invite-only rooms are joined with an invite code, which the room's host shares separately.
  • Rooms that are full are clearly marked as unavailable — you'll never accidentally try to cram into a packed room.
  • The room you are currently in is marked as such, with its Join button disabled, so the list always shows you exactly where you stand.

Every room also has an owner-configurable visual style — a coordinated palette for the backdrop, walls, and floor — chosen when the room is created. Styles range from a cool Modern Loft to a warm Cozy Den to more themed looks like Renaissance Studio and Candlelit Vault, which is exactly how the two showcase rooms covered later in this guide get their look before a single piece of furniture is placed.

construction Build mode

If you host a room, the Build Mode pill in the HUD unlocks room authoring. Flip it on and the Room Builder panel slides in, organized into three tabs — Furniture for placing objects, Room & Doors for managing tiles and navigation, and More for room-wide settings like the escape session and moderation. A grid-snap toggle sits alongside build mode to help you line objects up cleanly without fighting free-form pixel placement.

The catalog is deliberately broad, and every object type falls into one of a few families:

  • Furniture — table, chair, bar, sofa — sets the scene and gives visitors somewhere to gather or sit.
  • Media & knowledge — bookshelf, TV, music player — hold real content: books, videos, and tracks you author yourself.
  • AI character — a placeable story character with its own appearance, dialogue tree, knowledge base, and optional guided tour.
  • Escape-room objects — escape door, hidden item, and five puzzle-prop shapes (cipher box, digital lock, combination dial, riddle tablet, clue board) — build a timed, puzzle-gated challenge.

Placing an object is a drag from the catalog onto the room canvas. Once it's down, click it to select it and a small floating toolbar appears for that object alone, letting you move, rotate, recolor, fine-tune its settings (the tune icon), or delete it — all without leaving the room view. Everything you place is saved to the room immediately, so any other visitor sees it the moment you drop it.

check_circle The two sections below walk through building a complete educational room and a complete escape room from scratch, object by object — the fastest way to see how all of these pieces fit together.

smart_toy AI characters

Rooms can host AI story characters with their own appearance, scripted conversations, and a document-based knowledge base. Talking to one opens a dedicated dialogue window that keeps a running transcript of the whole conversation — your questions and the character's answers both stay on screen as you go deeper, rather than replacing the previous line every time you pick a new option.

Two modes govern how a character answers. In Predefined Mode, the character follows an author-written dialogue tree: a starting line, a set of clickable choices, and further nodes those choices lead to, optionally ending in a completion flag. If the room's host has connected a generative API, visitors can also type a free-form question into the ask box, and the character answers using its knowledge base for context — useful for open-ended "ask me anything" characters layered on top of a scripted core. Characters can also run guided tours: click "Follow me!" and the character walks a pre-authored path between waypoints, narrating as it goes, with you in tow.

school Building an educational room

A worked example, using the seeded room "Leonardo's Workshop."

An educational room turns the same objects covered above — furniture, a bookshelf, a TV, a music player, and an AI character — into a self-contained lesson. Leonardo's Workshop ships with the server as a live example: a three-tile Renaissance-styled room built entirely from the standard catalog, with no code beyond authoring content through the builder. Join it from the room chooser to explore everything described below firsthand.

palette Setting the scene with room style and tiles

The room starts from the Renaissance Studio visual style — ochre plaster, fresco-blue walls, a terracotta floor — chosen the moment the room was created, before a single object existed. Choosing a style is the fastest way to establish a vibe, because it colors the backdrop, walls, and floor consistently without having to color-match dozens of individual objects.

From there the room is split across three connected tiles, each with its own purpose: The Bottega (the workshop itself, with a worktable, stools, and a bench for conversation), Library & Codices (a reading room), and Hall of Inventions (a small gallery of plinths standing in for Leonardo's models and drawings). Splitting a lesson across tiles like this keeps each screen focused — a visitor reads in the library without the workshop's furniture crowding the view — while the Room & Doors tab in the builder lets you add, label, and connect tiles in exactly this way for your own rooms.

menu_book The bookshelf: your primary lesson content

The bookshelf is where an educational room does most of its teaching. Leonardo's Workshop stocks one with seven short, self-contained articles — his early life, the Verrocchio apprenticeship, the Milan years, the Mona Lisa and sfumato, the notebooks, his anatomical studies, and his final years in France — each with a title, an author credit, a one-line summary, and an estimated read time. Opening the shelf shows every book in a sidebar next to the reading pane, so a visitor can jump between articles without losing their place on the shelf, and their reading progress on each book is saved automatically as they scroll.

Authoring a book is one form in the builder's Furniture tab: place a bookshelf, select it, and use "Add Book to Shelf" to give it a title, an optional author and summary, an estimated read time, and the content itself — either plain paragraphs or a small markdown subset (headers, bold, italic) for longer articles. There's no limit tied to a single book beyond what's comfortable to read in one sitting; add as many books as your lesson needs.

movie TV and music: video and audio without leaving the room

A TV and a music player round out the media side of the lesson. The workshop's TV carries four short documentaries and explainer videos (each just a YouTube link pasted into the "Add Video" form), and its music player carries four period-appropriate tracks — Renaissance lute and choral pieces — so the room has an ambient soundtrack a visitor can put on while they read or explore. Both work exactly like the bookshelf: a sidebar of everything on the playlist next to the currently playing item, with a "Watch Together" option on the TV so a group can stay in sync on the same video.

smart_toy The AI character as guide and interviewee

The centerpiece of the room is an AI character configured as Leonardo da Vinci himself, using the historical persona role so he answers in character rather than as a neutral narrator. His knowledge base holds six short reference documents covering his life, paintings, notebooks, and anatomical work, and his dialogue tree opens with a welcome line and branches into topics a visitor can pick — his paintings, his flying-machine designs, his mirror handwriting, his final years — each answer written in his voice and drawing on the same facts as the bookshelf, so the two reinforce each other instead of repeating verbatim.

He can also run a short guided tour of the Bottega, walking a visitor between his worktable, the music player, and the bench before pointing them toward the library — a nice way to open a lesson before turning a visitor loose to read and explore on their own.

tips_and_updates Recipe for your own educational room: pick a themed room style, split the topic across a couple of tiles if it has distinct sections, put your core written material on a bookshelf, add supporting video/audio if you have it, then configure one AI character as the subject of the lesson with a knowledge base drawn from the same sources as your books.

meeting_room Building an escape room

A worked example, using the seeded room "The Alchemist's Vault."

An escape room layers a ticking clock and a chain of puzzles on top of the same builder tools. Join The Alchemist's Vault from the room chooser to try the finished example: a two-tile Candlelit Vault-styled room — the darkest of the visual styles, so hidden objects genuinely read as hidden — where four puzzles gate a locked door behind a twenty-minute timer.

timer The escape session: turning on the clock

The escape timer itself is a single room-wide setting, found in the builder's More tab as Configure Escape Session: flip it on, set a time limit, and write a short briefing that visitors see when they start. The Alchemist's Vault uses a twenty-minute limit and a briefing that sets up the whole premise — a locked study, four puzzles, an apprentice who can help. Team mode can also be turned on so a group shares one countdown and one win condition instead of racing each other individually.

extension Puzzle props: five shapes, three answer formats

Every puzzle needs a physical prop to attach to, and the catalog offers five purpose-built shapes — cipher box, digital lock, combination dial, riddle tablet, and clue board — so the room can visually vary how each puzzle presents itself instead of every clue looking like the same keypad. Place the prop first, then use "Add Puzzle" and bind it to that object.

A puzzle's answer can be checked three different ways, chosen per-puzzle to fit the clue:

  • Exact — the answer must match a specific word or phrase precisely (good for named techniques or terms).
  • Numeric — the answer is compared as a number, so formatting quirks don't matter (good for dates and years).
  • Contains — correct as long as the guess contains the key phrase (good for place names that might be phrased a few different ways).

Every puzzle also gets its own prompt text, up to a few graduated hints a stuck visitor can request, and an optional maximum number of attempts. Clicking a puzzle prop opens a small dialog with the prompt and an answer box — solving it is immediate and satisfying, with no separate confirmation step.

In the Vault, all four puzzles are individually numeric or exact-matched and are each tied to a fact from Leonardo's life — his birth year on a combination dial, the name of his painting technique on a cipher box, the year he documented the laws of friction on a digital lock, and the town he died in on a riddle tablet — so solving the escape room and reading the educational room reward the same knowledge from two different angles.

key Hidden items and the escape door

Some puzzles reward more than satisfaction: solving the Vault's cipher box reveals a hidden key object that was invisible until that moment, discouraging visitors from just wandering around looking for it before earning it. The escape door object is then configured, in "Configure Door," to require both that revealed key and all four puzzles solved — so every clue in the room genuinely gates the exit rather than being an optional side quest.

support_agent An AI character as in-room hint-giver

The Vault also places an AI character — Salaì, the apprentice — configured with the guide role and a knowledge base that summarizes what each of the four locks wants, without spelling out the answers outright. Talking to him is a safety net for a stuck group: he'll point you toward which puzzle to try first and what kind of fact it's looking for, while still leaving the actual solving to you.

tips_and_updates Recipe for your own escape room: turn on the escape session with a time limit and briefing, place a few puzzle props and give each one a prompt, hints, and the answer format that fits, wire at least one puzzle to reveal a hidden item, configure your escape door to require the puzzles (and any hidden items) you want, and place a guide character if you'd like visitors to have somewhere to turn when they're stuck.
